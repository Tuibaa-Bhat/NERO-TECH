Section 2 · Biology

The Biological Neuron

A neuron is a cell built to carry messages. It receives signals, decides whether they are strong enough, and passes an electrical pulse along a long fibre to the next cell.

One signal, step by step

  1. Arrival at the dendrites. Other neurons release chemical messengers (neurotransmitters). They bind to receptors on the dendrites and open tiny ion channels, creating a small voltage change.
  2. Adding up in the cell body. Thousands of small signals reach the soma. Excitatory signals push the cell toward firing, inhibitory ones push it away. The soma adds them all up.
  3. The threshold. The resting voltage is about −70 mV. If the total pushes it to about −55 mV, the neuron fires. Below that, nothing happens. This is all-or-nothing.
  4. The action potential. Sodium channels open and the voltage jumps to about +30 mV within a millisecond. Potassium channels reset it. This sharp pulse is the nerve impulse.
  5. Travel down the axon. In myelinated fibres the impulse jumps between the gaps called nodes of Ranvier. This can reach around 100 metres per second.
  6. Crossing the synapse. Calcium makes vesicles release neurotransmitter across a gap of about 20 nanometres. The chemical starts a new signal in the next cell.

Figures are typical textbook values. Check them against your own book before you submit.

Section 3 · Computer Science

The Artificial Neural Network

An artificial neural network is a set of simple maths units arranged in layers. Each unit multiplies its inputs by weights, adds a bias, and produces one number. Many units together can recognise patterns.

The three layers

  • Input layer (x1, x2, x3): the data going in, such as pixel brightness or a sensor reading.
  • Hidden layer (h1, h2, h3): each neuron combines the inputs its own way, so each one detects a different pattern.
  • Output layer (y): the final answer, always between 0 and 1.

How to read the picture

  • Blue lines are positive weights (they encourage the next neuron).
  • Magenta lines are negative weights (they discourage it).
  • Thicker, brighter lines mean bigger weights. Brighter neurons mean higher values.
  • The yellow dots are signals. They move from inputs to hidden neurons, then to the output.

What do the formulas mean? (plain English)
z = w1·x1 + w2·x2 + w3·x3 + b

Multiply each input by its weight (how much it matters), add them up, then add the bias b (a shift).

a = σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(−z))

Squash the number into 0 to 1. Large positive gives near 1, very negative gives near 0, zero gives 0.5.

y = σ(v1·h1 + v2·h2 + v3·h3 + b2)

The same idea for the output, using the hidden values h1, h2, h3 and the output weights v.

error = (y − target)²

How far the answer is from what we want. Zero is perfect.

new weight = old weight − 0.5 × (its share of the error)

Learning nudges each weight a little in the direction that makes the error smaller. This is backpropagation.

Section 5 · Brain vs Computer

The Human Brain

The model on the right is a simplified brain. Drag to turn it. The pink folds are the cortex, the outer layer where most thinking happens.

Main parts (labelled in 3D)

  • Frontal lobe: planning, decisions, personality and speech production.
  • Parietal lobe: touch, body position and handling numbers.
  • Temporal lobe: hearing, language and memory.
  • Occipital lobe: vision.
  • Cerebellum: balance and smooth movement.
  • Brainstem: breathing, heartbeat and sleep.

Parallel

About 86 billion neurons work at the same time.

Low power

Roughly 20 watts for the whole brain.

Learns by rewiring

Synapses strengthen or weaken with use.

Damage tolerant

It can lose parts and keep working, often with gradual decline.

Section 5 · Brain vs Computer

The Computer Neural Network

On the right is a computer neural network. The chip at the top is the processor (CPU or GPU). It works through the layers one after another, doing multiply-and-add operations very quickly.

How it differs

  • Fast arithmetic: billions of exact operations per second.
  • Power hungry: one high-end GPU can use hundreds of watts.
  • Learns by backpropagation: weights change using calculus and the output error.
  • Separate memory: weights live in memory and move to the processor.
  • Designed, not grown: engineers choose the layers and their sizes.

Fast

Exact numbers, billions of operations a second.

Power hungry

Hundreds of watts per GPU; data centres use megawatts.
